03One beat, part by part

Click any part of the beat or the buttons below. The shaded band shows exactly where it starts and ends.

Linking to the action potential and contractionA ventricular action potential maps as: upstroke at the QRS → calcium plateau between QRS and T (why the ST segment is flat) → repolarisation at the T wave. The ECG is electrical only, so you can't assume contraction from it, but each mechanical event always comes after its wave: P → atrial contraction, QRS → ventricular contraction, T → relaxation. Sinoatrial node to ventricles ≈ 0.15 s memorise.

04Vectors: why waves go up or down

Each lead has a "positive" end. It is only a convention (not a positive electrode like a battery): it is the end that reports.

Towards the positive end → trace goes up.

Away → trace goes down. Why P and QRS are inverted in aVR: it looks from the right shoulder and activity spreads away from it.

At ~90° → records little. The smallest angle (lead parallel to the event) gives the biggest signal.

So why 12 leads?A lead at ~90° to an event sees little of it and the direction of activity shifts, so you need several viewpoints. Hypertrophy of one side shifts the net activity, so the ECG appears to rotate in the chest. Lead II is most in line with normal spread, hence the lead II rhythm strip.

07The 12 steps

Open a step for what to check; tick it off as you go when practising on a real ECG.

Worked example: the 21-year-old pilotRate ~50–60 (R–R 5–6 large squares; 50, 54 or 60 accepted, 70 wrong) · irregular but sinus = sinus arrhythmia · P normal, PR 3–4 small squares · QRS 2.5–3 small squares, no S wave · no abnormal Q · R in V5 >25 mm (heavy workouts, not hypertension) · ST slightly scooped but normal for him · T upright even in V1–V3 · normal axis → normal ECG for this patient.

Your Clinical Skills deck orders it slightly differently (rhythm before rate, ST before QT, axis at step 11). Either works if you're consistent.

Sinus rhythm memoriseNormal P waves · normal narrow QRS · one P before every QRS · regular. The first three make it sinus; irregular can still be sinus (sinus arrhythmia).
Regular? Paper methodMark 3 successive R waves on paper, slide along twice, check gaps match. A mismatch of ≤1 small square still counts.

10Tachycardias

Tachycardia sorter
Answer two questions; ▶ plays the rhythm in the monitor
VT or supraventricular with aberration?QRS >140 ms, independent P waves, fusion beats → VT (cannon waves in the JVP). Broad complex + previous left ventricular damage → statistically VT. Fast and unwell: DC cardioversion either way.
Adenosine testBlocks the AV node to unveil the rhythm. Transient slowing → flutter or atrial tachycardia · sinus rhythm restored → re-entry · no effect → wrong diagnosis (sinus tachycardia, or VT if broad).
Re-entry memoriseWrite "circus" for re-entrant tachycardia. AVNRT: circuit entirely around the AV node; P waves hidden in the QRS or retrograde in II, III, aVF. AVRT: over an accessory pathway and the AV node.

12Infarction

Suspected acute coronary syndrome: the ECG is the first and most important investigation memorise. Non-diagnostic? Repeat it in ~10 minutes: it changes within minutes.

STEMI evolution

STEMI localiser
Pick a territory: red = ST elevation, blue = ST depression

ST elevation = infarction>2 mm in 2 adjacent chest leads, or >1 mm in 2 adjacent limb leads. Complete blockage → transmural (STEMI). Very high square elevation = tombstoning. Elevation in some leads with depression in others = reciprocal change.
ST depressionIncomplete blockage = ischaemia: angina (resolves) or NSTEMI (troponin raised; subendocardial). Exception: V1–V2 only = posterior infarction. Treadmill: isoelectric at rest → depressed on exercise.
Posterior memoriseNo lead faces the back: reciprocal changes in V1, ST depression + tall R. Usually right coronary artery.
Pulmonary embolism: S1 Q3 T3 memoriseLarge S in I · deep Q in III · inverted T in III. Only ~30%: needs a massive embolism (strain on the heart).
